Problem

Product Managers at mid-size, high-growth tech companies attend multiple recurring stakeholder meetings daily. Without a reliable system to track what was decided, left open, and promised in each relationship, PMs walk in without context. Conversations repeat. Commitments are missed. Stakeholder trust erodes. Current tools require manual input, are reactive, or are locked to a single enterprise stack. No tool today silently builds a living memory of PM stakeholder relationships and surfaces context automatically — before the meeting begins.

My Role

Full product ownership — from problem framing and competitive analysis through persona definition, success metrics, user story writing, MoSCoW prioritisation, and risk documentation. This is original zero-to-one product work built as part of my PM transition portfolio. No existing product to inherit — problem first, solution second.

Use Case 1 — Pre-Meeting Briefing Alert

User Story: As a Product Manager, I want to receive a proactive briefing 10 minutes before a recurring stakeholder meeting, so that I can walk in prepared without spending time on manual prep.
AC1 — Trigger: Given a recurring stakeholder meeting exists, when it is 10 minutes prior, then the product triggers and delivers an alert to the PM.
AC2 — Content: Given an alert has been raised, when the briefing is generated, then it must contain the previous meeting summary, open action items, commitments made, and relationship tone — specific to that meeting.
AC3 — Load state: Given the alert has been delivered and when the tool is clicked, then the briefing is ready and displays the full content accurately.

Use Case 2 — Data Privacy and User Control

User Story: As a Product Manager, I want to be able to view what data BriefBack is storing of my meeting recordings, emails, or Slack messages so that I can check for anything highly confidential and delete it.
AC1 — Data view: Given Priya is logged into BriefBack, when she navigates to the data storage view, then the system displays all stored data including meeting summaries, email threads, and Slack messages associated with her account.
AC2 — Deletion: Given the data view is available, when Priya selects a specific piece of stored data to delete, then the system permanently deletes the selected data and displays a confirmation message.
AC3 — Confirmation timing: Given Priya has deleted a piece of data, when deletion is completed, then the system confirms permanent deletion within five seconds.

Use Case 3 — Silent Meeting Participation

User Story: As a Product Manager, I want the bot to join the meeting alongside me so that it silently summarises the conversation and keeps it ready for the next recurring meeting with the same stakeholder.
AC1 — Join: Given the bot has calendar access, when a meeting is scheduled, then the bot silently joins the meeting alongside the user 95% of the time.
AC2 — Recording: Given the bot is present in the meeting, when the meeting is ongoing, then the bot silently records and processes the conversation accurately.
AC3 — Briefing delivery: Given the bot has stored the meeting conversation, when the meeting ends, then the bot summarises the discussion within five minutes and delivers a complete briefing — including Slack messages and email context — ten minutes before the next recurring stakeholder meeting.

NFRs define the quality standards BriefBack must meet. These are not features — they describe how well the system must perform for the product to be trusted and adopted.

NFR 1 — Security and Authorisation: The system must obtain explicit OAuth permissions from Zoom, Slack, and Gmail to access meeting transcripts, messages, and email threads. All data must be encrypted at rest and in transit using AES-256 standard. Users must retain the right to revoke access and delete all stored data within 24 hours of requesting deletion.
NFR 2 — Bot Reliability and Availability: The bot must successfully join 95% of scheduled recurring stakeholder meetings, and must notify the PM within 2 minutes if it fails to join — so the PM is not left without context unknowingly.
NFR 3 — AI Output Accuracy: The AI-generated briefing must achieve a minimum accuracy rate of 85% on commitments, action items, and meeting summaries — measured through monthly user feedback ratings. The system must flag low-confidence outputs to the PM rather than presenting uncertain content as fact.

These assumptions underpin the PRD. If any is found to be incorrect during engineering review, the impacted sections must be revisited before build begins.

1
OAuth permissions can be obtained for API access across Zoom, Slack, Gmail, and Google Calendar.
2
A suitable AI model exists that can accurately extract meeting summaries, commitments, tone, and context from unstructured meeting data, email threads, and Slack messages.
3
The engineering team can build or procure encrypted data storage that meets AES-256 standard.
4
PMs can grant authorisation access to BriefBack without requiring full IT approval at an individual level.

Key Risks

R1 — Data Privacy and Security: BriefBack accesses meeting recordings, emails, and calendar data. A breach would damage trust, create legal liability, and likely end the product. Mitigation: end-to-end encryption, clear data retention policies, and user-controlled deletion must ship at MVP.
R2 — Enterprise IT Approval: Individual PMs may want BriefBack but IT policies may block third-party tools accessing email and calendar data. Mitigation: build data privacy controls into MVP and prepare enterprise security documentation early in the GTM process.

Open Questions

  • What AI model and data pipeline architecture is required to accurately extract commitments and relationship tone from unstructured meeting data?
  • Will BriefBack's differentiation on proactive relationship intelligence sustain adoption against Copilot, Otter.ai, and Google Workspace AI?
  • Do Zoom, Slack, Gmail, and Google Calendar API policies permit the level of data access BriefBack requires at scale?
Case Study 2 Process Automation · FinTech

APPIA Key Provisioning
Automation

Blue Prism RPA  ·  SS&C Technologies  ·  Internal Platform

~6 months  ·  2025
2–3 hrs → under 5 min per request
90% reduction in D-station dependency
95% form submission accuracy
Same-day processing for all 10–15 weekly requests
Problem

The APPIA key provisioning process at SS&C was entirely manual. Project Managers sent unstructured emails. The D-station team — reduced from 10 members to 4 — spent 2–3 hours per request manually fetching client data from Salesforce, filling forms by hand, and emailing a third-party provider. With 10–15 requests arriving weekly, this created recurring bottlenecks, frequent errors, and consistent burnout across all four stakeholder groups.

My Role

I identified this process on SS&C's internal Bright Ideas automation board, initiated discovery across four stakeholder groups, led solution definition, drove cross-functional delivery with the RPA development team, and managed post-launch adoption issues — including establishing escalation protocols and enforcing input standards with Project Managers.

Discovery involved structured conversations with four stakeholder groups: the D-station team manager, D-station members, Project Managers, and a subset of clients who had experienced provisioning delays.

Evidence gathered: emails showing PM and client frustration, direct burnout accounts from D-station members, observable two-week turnaround data, and error patterns flagged by the third-party provider. The evidence was consistent across all groups — making the problem impossible to dismiss as isolated.

The root trigger was not the process itself changing. It was the same broken process meeting a team with a quarter of its original capacity.

Blue Prism RPA was selected after a cross-functional alignment call. The process was repetitive, rule-based, and stable — a textbook automation candidate. The key product decision was standardising the input format. Blue Prism cannot interpret free-form emails, so a tabular email template was designed and introduced for all Project Managers.

Bot workflow end to end:

  • PM sends standardised email to Blue Prism user ID
  • Bot reads structured data, opens Salesforce, fetches all required client fields
  • Bot fills APPIA key request form automatically
  • Bot emails third-party provider with form attached — requestor CC'd directly
  • Requestor receives the key without D-station acting as a relay
Agreed trade-off: if a PM submitted an incorrectly formatted email, the bot would fail on that request. PMs were required to correct and resend — not D-station. This was explicit and documented upfront.

Issue 1 — Broken escalation protocol: D-station was escalating bot failures directly to engineering without first diagnosing the cause. Engineers were spending time on what were actually simple input errors. I established a clear protocol: verify email format and client details before reaching engineering. This alone significantly reduced unnecessary escalations.

Issue 2 — PMs reverting to old behaviour: Some PMs continued sending unstructured emails post-launch. D-station began correcting these quietly to avoid friction — absorbing work that was not theirs. I stopped this. PMs were required to correct and resend their own emails. A deliberate decision to reinforce the standard and prevent D-station from regressing to the old pattern.

The biggest gap was communication breadth. Leadership-level alignment was strong — but the message did not reach all Project Managers uniformly. Some received the new template with full context. Others got a diluted version through indirect channels. The result was predictable: incorrect email formats continued post-launch.

If I ran this again: after leadership alignment, one all-hands session with every participant — not just senior members. One message, one room, one moment of shared understanding. Automation projects fail at the edges, and the edges are usually the people who were not in the original room.
